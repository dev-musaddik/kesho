# Why Your Aesthetic Practitioner's Qualifications Matter (and What to Ask Before Booking)

If you have been researching aesthetic treatments for any length of time, you have almost certainly come across the headlines. Hospitalisations from botched filler. "Botox parties" gone wrong. Save Face reporting complaints that have doubled year on year. It is unsettling — and it should be.

The uncomfortable reality is that in the UK, almost anyone can buy a needle, take a weekend course, and start injecting members of the public the following Monday. There is no legal requirement to be medically trained. The same person who tints your eyebrows can — quite legally — put dermal filler in your tear trough.

This guide is here to help you cut through it. Not to scare you off treatment, but to make sure you know what to look for, what to ask, and what to walk away from. By the end, you will understand the aesthetic practitioner qualifications UK clinics actually need, the difference between the various tiers, and the eight questions that will tell you almost everything you need to know about a clinic in under five minutes.

## The uncomfortable truth: UK aesthetics is largely unregulated

Let us start with the headline fact, because most people genuinely do not know it.

In the United Kingdom, the non-surgical aesthetics industry — Botox, fillers, skin boosters, polynucleotides, fat dissolving, microneedling — is not a regulated profession. There is no central register you must be on, no legally mandated minimum qualification, no requirement to hold insurance, and no obligation to keep medical records or report adverse events.

This is not the case in most comparable countries. In much of Europe, injectables are restricted to doctors. In the UK, dermal filler is currently classified as a medical device rather than a medicine — the legal loophole that allows any adult to inject it.

The good news is that this is changing. The 2023 Health and Care Act introduced a licensing scheme for non-surgical cosmetic procedures in England, and as of 2026 implementation is being phased in across treatment categories. But "phased in" is doing a lot of work in that sentence — meaningful protection is still some way off, and in the meantime the responsibility for finding a safe practitioner sits squarely with you.

## What "regulated" actually means in this industry

When clinics use the word "regulated", they can mean very different things, and the difference matters.

A **regulated healthcare professional** is someone whose practice is overseen by a statutory UK body: the General Medical Council (GMC) for doctors, the General Dental Council (GDC) for dentists, the Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) for nurses, or the General Pharmaceutical Council (GPhC) for pharmacists. These regulators set training standards, maintain a public register, investigate complaints, and can strike a practitioner off for misconduct.

An **OfQual-regulated qualification** is something different but also meaningful. OfQual is the UK qualifications regulator. An OfQual-regulated Level 7 qualification in injectables is the highest non-medical training pathway in aesthetics — broadly equivalent in academic level to a master's degree — and is what serious independent practitioners hold when they are not also doctors, dentists, nurses, or pharmacists. It is a marker of assessed competence rather than a weekend certificate.

A **voluntary register** like the Joint Council for Cosmetic Practitioners (JCCP) or Save Face is a third layer. These are not statutory regulators, but they do verify credentials, insurance, and premises before listing a practitioner. Save Face in particular is government-approved and is one of the most useful tools you have as a patient.

## The five tiers of practitioner — from least to most qualified

Not all "aesthetic practitioners" are created equal. Here is the honest hierarchy.

### 1. Beauty therapist with a weekend foundation course

A two-day course, an attendance certificate, and a needle. No medical background, no anatomy training beyond the basics, and crucially no ability to prescribe or hold the emergency drugs needed if something goes wrong. This is the tier that drives most of the headlines — and the issue is the system that allows it, not the individual practitioners, many of whom genuinely believed the course they took was sufficient.

### 2. Aesthetic practitioner — varies wildly, context matters

This is the broadest category and the most confusing. "Aesthetic practitioner" is not a protected title. The person using it could be anything from a self-taught injector to a clinician with twenty years of training. You have to ask follow-up questions — the title alone tells you almost nothing.

### 3. Aesthetic practitioner with OfQual Level 7 regulation

A genuine training pathway with assessed anatomy, pharmacology, complication management, and supervised practice. Combined with experience and a working relationship with a prescriber, this is a strong tier.

### 4. Nurse, dentist or pharmacist independent prescriber

A regulated healthcare professional (NMC, GDC, or GPhC) who has completed additional independent prescribing qualifications. They can legally prescribe prescription-only medicines, including botulinum toxin and the emergency drug hyaluronidase, and hold and administer these drugs on site.

### 5. GMC-registered doctor

A medically qualified doctor on the General Medical Council register. Full diagnostic training, prescribing rights, and accountable to the GMC. A doctor's value is most obvious in complex cases, complication management, and overall clinical oversight.

The point of laying these out is not that only tier five should ever inject — it is that you should know which tier you are sitting in front of, and what that means for your safety.

## Why "prescriber on site" is non-negotiable for injectables

Botulinum toxin — the active ingredient in Botox, Azzalure, Bocouture, Letybo and similar brands — is a Prescription-Only Medicine (POM). By UK law, it can only be prescribed by a doctor, dentist, pharmacist independent prescriber, or nurse independent prescriber, following a face-to-face consultation with the patient.

In practice, the industry has spent years using a workaround known as "remote prescribing", where a prescriber the patient has never met signs off on the prescription based on a form or a quick video call. The regulators — including the GPhC and the NMC — have made clear they expect this to end. Best practice, and increasingly the only acceptable practice, is for the prescriber to see you in person before any POM is administered.

Why does this matter beyond the legal technicality? Because the prescriber is also the person who can deal with things going wrong. If you have a vascular occlusion from filler — where filler is injected into or compresses a blood vessel — you have a narrow window to dissolve it using **hyaluronidase**, itself a POM. A clinic without a prescriber on site cannot legally hold hyaluronidase. If something goes wrong at 2pm on a Saturday, the difference between a prescriber being in the next room and one being on the end of a phone is the difference between a good outcome and a permanent one.

This is why, when we talk about [anti-wrinkle treatment](/treatments/anti-wrinkle/) at KESHO, the prescriber consultation is not an optional add-on — it is the treatment.

## Eight questions to ask any clinic before booking

Print this list. Read it out on the phone. A good clinic will answer all eight without hesitation. If any answer is vague, defensive, or "don't worry about that", treat it as your answer.

1. **"Is your practitioner OfQual-regulated, or NMC, GMC, GDC, or GPhC registered?"** You want a specific named registration body and ideally a registration number you can verify on the public register.
2. **"Who is the prescriber for my treatment, and are they on site today?"** For any prescription-only medicine, the prescriber should see you in person on the day. "Our prescriber works remotely" is no longer acceptable practice.
3. **"What is the product, brand, and batch number — and can I see it before treatment?"** Reputable clinics use known brands, source them through proper medical wholesalers, and are happy to show you the unopened box.
4. **"What is your emergency and vascular occlusion protocol?"** They should be able to describe it in plain English: how they spot an occlusion, who reverses it, how quickly, and what happens afterwards.
5. **"Do you carry hyaluronidase on site, and who can administer it?"** The answer should be yes, and the named administrator should be a prescriber.
6. **"What aftercare support is included, and how do I reach you if I am worried?"** You want a direct line, not a generic inbox checked on Mondays.
7. **"What is the consent and cooling-off process?"** Best practice is a consultation separated from treatment by a cooling-off period, with written consent and clear information on risks.
8. **"What insurance does the practitioner hold, and with which insurer?"** Specialist aesthetic indemnity insurance is essential. A vague "yes, we are insured" is not enough.

## Red flags — when to walk away

There is no single deal-breaker, but several of the following together should have you reaching for your coat.

- Treatment offered the same day as the first contact, with no separate consultation
- Heavy discounting, time-limited "flash sales" on injectables, or buy-one-get-one offers
- Treatment in a home, hotel room, salon backroom, or pop-up
- Reluctance to name the prescriber or show product packaging
- "Don't worry, I have been doing this for years" in place of an actual answer
- Pressure to book more than you came in for
- No written aftercare instructions and no clear complications pathway

None of these on their own makes someone unsafe. All of them together is a pattern.

## The Save Face register and how to use it

Save Face is a government-approved register of accredited aesthetic practitioners in the UK. To be listed, a practitioner has to evidence their qualifications, insurance, premises, prescribing arrangements, and complications protocols, and they are re-audited annually.

For patients, the value is simple. You can search the register by postcode, see who has been independently vetted, and read verified reviews. It is not the only marker of a good clinic — plenty of excellent practitioners are also JCCP-listed — but it is one of the easiest sanity checks you can run before booking anywhere.

## Upcoming UK regulation: the 2023 Health and Care Act

The Health and Care Act 2022 gave the Secretary of State powers to introduce a licensing scheme for non-surgical cosmetic procedures in England, and that scheme is being implemented in phases through the mid-2020s. In broad terms, it will require practitioners to hold a personal licence, premises to hold a premises licence, and higher-risk procedures to be restricted to qualified healthcare professionals.

This is genuinely good news, and the most significant move toward proper regulation the UK aesthetics industry has ever seen. In the meantime, the questions in this guide are how you bridge the gap.
